The 1-year recaulk cycle
isn't a caulk problem. It's a prep problem.

Search around and you'll find wildly different answers for how often caulk needs replacing — some say every year, some say five to ten. Both are true, for different jobs. The one-year cycle is what happens when old caulk gets scraped off (or just caulked over) with moisture, mold, and soap scum still trapped behind it. A five-to-ten-year lifespan is possible when the surface is actually cleaned, dried, and treated before anything new goes on. Same tube of caulk. Completely different outcome.

Where the Mold Actually Comes From

It's not on top of the caulk — it's underneath it

Without proper removal of the old caulk, grout, and any existing mold — and a thorough cleaning with alcohol — trapped moisture and mold are likely to keep growing underneath. Inadequate cleaning also leaves poor adhesion, which gives water and mold an opening to gain a foothold.

Smeared or too-thin caulk sections are an additional weak point. Instead of forming a solid, flexible seal, thin caulk is prone to lifting slightly at the edge — especially from everyday tile cleaning — and that lifted edge is exactly where mold gets its foothold. Once it takes hold, it has room to grow, which is when you see that telltale black line forming. What you're actually looking at in most moldy corners isn't mold sitting on the caulk — it's mold growing in the gap between the caulk and the tile.

Two Different Jobs

"Caulking" isn't one service — it's two

A brand-new kitchen and a ten-year-old kitchen need genuinely different work, even though both end with a bead of caulk. The real question isn't which one you have — it's whether the last recaulk actually removed everything underneath, or just covered it up.

Knowing this now — did you ever get the feeling there wasn't much prep work behind your last caulk job? That's usually exactly what happened.

What We Check First

Corners need caulk, not grout —
and few do this right

Anywhere two flat surfaces meet at an angle — the counter meeting the backsplash, the wall meeting the tub — that joint moves. Materials expand and contract, and the two surfaces never move in perfect unison. Grout is rigid; it cracks when the joint underneath it shifts. Caulk is flexible; it moves with the joint instead of fighting it. Any inside corner needs caulk, not grout, or you'll eventually see a hairline crack right along that line.

In our field experience, roughly 9 out of 10 tile jobs run grout straight through the corners anyway — because it's faster to keep going with the same grout line than to stop, tool a clean edge, and caulk the transition separately. It's one of the first things we check and correct on every job.

~90% of jobs we inspect have grout where caulk belongs

Right Product, Right Zone

Not all caulk is the same tube

Wet Zones

100% Silicone

Truly waterproof and flexible. The only correct choice for anywhere that sits in standing water or gets wet daily — sink rims, tub/shower joints, backsplash-to-counter seams.

Properly prepped: 10–20 years

Siliconized Acrylic

A blended compromise — more water-resistant than plain acrylic, but less durable than 100% silicone, and paint doesn't always adhere evenly. Fine for some trim work; the wrong call for anything wet.

Dry interior use: moderate lifespan

Acrylic (Latex)

Paintable and easy to clean up — the right choice for baseboards, window casing, and trim that never sees water. In a wet area it can fail in as little as 1–3 years.

Dry interior use: 5–10 years

Straight Answers

Questions we get about caulking

How often do I actually need to recaulk?

It depends entirely on how it was done last time. Poorly-prepped caulk (nothing removed, moisture trapped underneath) commonly fails within a year. Properly removed, treated, and resealed caulk typically holds for five years or more. The "how often" question is really a "how well was it done" question.

Why does my caulk keep growing mold back after only a year?

Because the mold was never actually removed — just sealed back over. A new bead of caulk on top of an untreated surface traps whatever was already growing underneath it. Full removal and treatment before resealing is the only way to break that cycle.

Can you just caulk over the old caulk instead of removing it?

No — and this is worth knowing before you hire anyone who offers to. Silicone in particular won't bond reliably to old caulk of any kind, whether it's old silicone or old acrylic. Skipping removal means a seal that looks fine on day one and fails early.

What's the difference between silicone, siliconized acrylic, and plain acrylic caulk?

Silicone is fully waterproof and flexible — correct for anything that gets wet. Acrylic is paintable and easy to clean up — correct for trim and baseboards that stay dry. Siliconized acrylic tries to split the difference and ends up a compromise on both fronts. Simple rule: silicone for anything wet, acrylic for anything painted.

Why do corners need caulk instead of grout?

Any place where two flat surfaces meet at an angle — an inside corner — moves slightly as materials expand, contract, and the house settles. Grout is rigid and cracks under that movement. Caulk is flexible and moves with it. Corners get caulk; flat runs get grout.

Can I paint over caulk?

Only acrylic (latex) caulk takes paint reliably. 100% silicone will not accept paint at all, and siliconized acrylic can accept it inconsistently. If a joint needs to disappear under paint, it needs to be acrylic — which is also why silicone is never used on trim that's meant to be painted.

Should the caulk match my grout color?

Generally, yes — a color-matched caulk line at the transition between grout and caulk is far less noticeable. We account for this when selecting product so the corner seal reads as a continuation of the grout line, not a visible patch. One tip if you're shopping around: standard white and almond caulk are usually in the paint aisle, but custom-matched colors are typically stocked with tile supplies instead. Some boutique tile stores will hand you a color sample — just know that's a molded plastic chip meant to represent the shade, not an actual cured caulk sample, so the real bead can read slightly differently.

Aren't caulking and grouting the same thing?

No. Grout is rigid and fills the flat gaps between tiles. Caulk is flexible and seals the corners and transitions where movement happens. Using the wrong one in the wrong place is one of the most common — and most avoidable — mistakes we find.

Is caulking a brand-new kitchen the same job as recaulking an older one?

No — they're different enough that we price and scope them differently. A new kitchen needs a clean, defined pass with no removal involved. An older kitchen typically needs full removal, mold treatment, and a dry-out period before resealing. See the two-track breakdown above.
